Analyze & Interpret the Model

Read Sections 1.2-4

Once we have converted our question into a clearly stated, well-defined math problem, we can begin to analyze it.

Step 2: Analyze the Model

Just like in calculus, we use a three-pronged approach to analyze differential equations:

  • Qualitative Approach. What does the equation governing the slope of the model tell us about the properties of any solution? Can we sketch a graph of the solution? Can we determine the long-run behavior of a solution? How do the properties of a solution depend on the parameters in the model?
  • Numerical Approach. Is there a numerical method we can use to estimate the values of a solution? Can we determine error bounds for this method? How does the error or convergence of the method depend on initial values or parameters?
  • Analytical Approach. Do we know a solution exists? Given some initial state, is the solution unique? Is it possible to actually extract a formula for the model?

Qualitative Approach - Slope Fields

Slope fields are one tool that we can use to try and understand the behavior of a solution \(y(t)\). To create a slope field for the equation \(\frac{dy}{dt}=f(t,y)\):

  • Select a grid of points in the \((t,y)\)-plane.
  • At each point \((t,y)\) in your grid, draw a small line through that point with slope \(f(t,y)\).
  • Each “slope” that you draw really represents a tangent line. Any solution passing through that point must be tangent to the line you draw at that point.
  • Once all your slopes are drawn, sit back and stare at it for a bit like a Magic Eye puzzle.

If \(f(t,y)\) isn’t too complicated, we can use the slope field to, either mentally or by hand, sketch out what a solution to the differential equation is going to look like. At the very least, try to see where a solution \(y(t)\) will be increasing/decreasing. You may also be able to see what happens to \(y(t)\) as \(t\to\infty\). Occasionally, you may even be able to guess formulas for simple solutions to the differential equation based on the slope field. (See below for an example of such a scenario.)

NoteExample - Slope Field

For example, the slope field for \(\frac{dy}{dt}=y-t\) is shown below:

Looking closely at the slope field, we can see that there is one particular line where all the slopes line up. This suggests that any solution starting on this line will stay on this line. The equation of that line is \(y=t+1\).

Is this actually a solution? Checking \(y(t)=t+1\): \[\begin{align} & y'(t)=1 \\ & y(t)-t = t+1 - t = 1 \end{align}\] We see that this satisfies the differential equation and is therefore a solution.

We can also see how other solutions will behave relative to the one we found. If a solution starts above the line \(y=t+1\), it will increase away from this line. If a solution starts just below \(y=t+1\), it will increase at first, but its slope will be less than 1 and so it will bend away from the line \(y=t+1\). Eventually, the solution will intersect the line \(y=t\), where all the values of \(dy/dt=0\); after this point, the solution will decrease.

Discussion Problem 1: Sprint Speed Continued

Recall that our model for a sprinter’s speed throughout a 100m race is given by: \[m\frac{dv}{dt}=mP-kmv\] where \(m\) is the sprinter’s mass (in kg), \(P\) represents the max effort acceleration a sprinter can achieve (in m/s\(^2\)), and \(k\) is the proportionality constant for the resistive force (in s\(^{-1}\)).

We will start by analyzing it qualitatively:

  1. Describe how the sprinter’s speed is expected to change throughout the race according to the model. Will it increase? decrease?
  2. Describe how the sprinter’s acceleration is expected to change throughout the race.
  3. Sketch a graph of \(v(t)\). What do you expect to happen to \(v(t)\) in the long run, i.e. what is \[\lim_{t\to\infty}v(t)?\] What is the practical significance of this?
TipAnswer:

First, it helps to simplify the differential equation by dividing through by mass: \[\frac{dv}{dt}=P-kv\]

  1. Note that \(\frac{dv}{dt}=0\) when \(v=\frac{P}{k}\). So, that means if the sprinter is ever going at this speed, they will stay at this speed for all time. A constant solution to a differential equation, like this one, is called a equilibrium solution.

    If \(v>\frac{P}{k}\), then \(\frac{dv}{dt}<0\), and so at this speed, their speed will decrease.

    If \(v<\frac{P}{k}\), then \(\frac{dv}{dt}>0\), and so at this speed, their speed will increase.

    In context, their speed at the beginning of the race will be 0 and so this is telling us that their speed should increase throughout the race according to the model.

  2. If \(v\) is increasing throughout the race, then their acceleration \[\frac{dv}{dt}=P-kv\] will decrease.

  3. Here is a “sketch” of a solution using a slope or direction field.

This tells us that, as the race goes on, the runner’s speed will approach a threshold of \(P/k\). This tells us that, if a runner is looking to improve their performance, they need to figure out how to make \(P\) bigger or \(k\) smaller.

Numerical Approach - Euler’s Method

When sketching solutions using a slope field, we start at a particular point, then move forward by allowing the slopes to “push” the solution in a particular direction. Euler’s method makes this idea a little more precise by turning it in to a sequence of computations.

The main idea—to approximate a solution \(y(t)\) for the differential equation \[\frac{dy}{dt}=f(t,y):\]

  • Choose \((t_0, y_0)\), the starting point in the \((t,y)\)-plane. If you are solving an initial value problem, use the indicated starting point.
  • Specify an ending time \(t_N>t_0\).
  • Choose \(N\), the total number of steps you will be using to get from \(t_0\) to \(t_N\).
  • Use linear approximation to recursively generate a sequence of estimates \(y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_N\) for the values \(y(t_1), y(t_2), \ldots, y(t_N)\).
  • The piecewise linear function defined by the points \((t_0, y_0), (t_1,y_1), \ldots, (t_N,y_N)\) is then your approximation for the solution \(y(t)\) on the interval \(t_0\leq t\leq t_N\).
NoteExample - Euler’s Method

For example, we can apply this idea to approximate the solution of \[\frac{dy}{dt}=y-t\] on the interval \(0\leq t \leq 2\), assuming that \(y(0)=0.5\). Using four steps (\(N=4\)):

Euler’s Method, \(N=4\)
Step \(k\) \(t_k\) \(y_k\) \(dy/dt\bigr\rvert_{(t_k,y_k)}\)
0 \(0\) \(0.5\) \(0.5-0=0.5\)
1 \(0.5\) \(0.5+0.5\cdot 0.5 = 0.75\) \(0.75-0.5=0.25\)
2 \(1\) \(0.75+0.25\cdot0.5 = 0.875\) \(0.875-1= -0.125\)
3 \(1.5\) \(0.875 - 0.125\cdot0.5 = 0.8125\) \(0.8125-1.5 = -0.6875\)
4 \(2\) \(0.8125-0.6875\cdot 0.5 = 0.46875\) \(0.46875 - 2 = -1.53125\)

The graph below shows how the approximate solution (black) compares to the actual solution (red). The green curve illustrates the improvement in the approximate solution when we increase the number of steps used by a factor of 10.

This process can be stated more formally as follows:

Definition 1 (Euler’s Method) To create an approximate solution for the initial value problem \[\frac{dy}{dt}=f(t,y), \quad y(t_0)=y_0 \] on the interval \(t_0\leq t\leq t_N\):

Initialization: Choose \(N\), the number of steps that will be used in the approximation. This determines the time step for each step of the procedure, \(\Delta t = \frac{t_N-t_0}{N}\). The initial value \(y(t_0)=y_0\) and time interval for the approximate solution \(t_0\leq t\leq t_N\) may also need to be chosen if not given by the problem.

Computations: For \(k=1,\ldots N\), we approximate the value of \(y(t_k)\) by \[y(t_k)\approx y_k = y_{k-1}+f(t_{k-1},y_{k-1})\cdot \Delta t, \quad\text{where } t_k=t_{k-1}+\Delta t\]

Final Output: Once the \(y_k\) are computed, the approximate solution \(\widetilde{y}(t)\) is the piecewise linear function on \(t_0\leq t\leq t_N\) connecting the points \((t_k, y_k)\); that is, for \(k=1,\ldots N\), on the \(k\)-th subinterval: \[\widetilde{y}(t)=y_{k-1}+f(t_{k-1},y_{k-1})\cdot(t-t_{k-1}), \quad\text{for } t_{k-1}\leq t\leq t_k\]

Notice that our estimates for \(v(t)\) oscillate rather than climbing smoothly toward a limiting value, and even briefly overshoot the eventual equilibrium speed \(v=P/k\approx 11.76\) ms\(^{-1}\) by nearly double. This is an artifact of the step size we chose: with \(k\Delta t = 0.85(2) = 1.7\), our step is close to (but still comfortably below) the stability threshold \(k\Delta t = 2\) for this type of equation, so our estimates zig-zag back and forth while still gradually settling toward the equilibrium. A smaller step size would smooth this out considerably — try it and see!

Analytical Approach - Separation of Variables

The method of separation of variables is an example of one technique we can use to try and find formulas for solutions to differential equations. This technique only works on separable equations, that is equations that can be separated as the product of a function in \(t\) and a function in \(y\): \[\frac{dy}{dt} = f(t,y) = g(t)h(y)\]

The steps in this method are:

  • Separate. Move things around so that you have your \(y\) terms and \(t\) terms on separate sides of the equation \[\frac{1}{h(y)}dy = g(t)dt \]
  • Integrate. Find general antiderivatives for both sides of the equation. Since the constants of integration can be combined, we generally only write “+ C” on the time side: \[\int\frac{1}{h(y)}dy =\int g(t)dt \Rightarrow H(y) = G(t) + C \]
  • Isolate. Use algebra to get \(y\) by itself. \[y(t)= H^{-1}(G(t)+C)\]
  • Initial Condition. For initial value problems, substitute the initial condition \((t_0, y_0)\) into the equation in order to solve for \(C\).

Discussion Problem 3: Analytical Analysis.

Our model for how sprint speed will change throughout the race:

  1. Use separation of variables this technique to solve the equation.
  2. Once you have your general solution, try and solve the initial value problem, where we are looking for a solution to the equation such that \(v(0)=0\).
TipAnswer:

  1. Starting again from \(\frac{dv}{dt}=P-kv\), we separate variables: \[\frac{1}{P-kv}\,dv = dt\] Integrating both sides (folding the constant of integration into the time side): \[-\frac{1}{k}\ln|P-kv| = t+C\] Solving for \(v\): \[|P-kv| = e^{-k(t+C)} = e^{-kC}e^{-kt}\] Since \(e^{-kC}\) is just some positive constant, we can drop the absolute value by allowing our constant to take either sign: \[P-kv = Be^{-kt} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v(t) = \frac{P}{k}-\frac{B}{k}e^{-kt}\] Renaming \(B/k\) as our arbitrary constant \(A\), our general solution is \[v(t) = \frac{P}{k}-Ae^{-kt}\]

  2. Using the initial condition \(v(0)=0\): \[0 = \frac{P}{k}-A \quad\Rightarrow\quad A=\frac{P}{k}\] So the solution to our initial value problem is \[v(t) = \frac{P}{k}\left(1-e^{-kt}\right)\] Notice that as \(t\to\infty\), \(v(t)\to P/k\) — exactly what we predicted using our qualitative approach back in Discussion Problem 1!

Step 3: Interpret the Model

The results of your mathematical analysis ultimately have to be reframed in the context of your model. What did you learn? How does it relate to your motivating questions? Do your assumptions place any limitations on what you can reasonably take away from your analysis?

If possible, compare how well your model fits real data. What can you learn from any discrepancies and how can you use this information to make your model better?

Discussion Problem 4: Check your model against real data.

In this case, we are modeling a relationship between real-world variables and so we can check how well our model performs against real data. In the 2024 Summer Olympics, Julien Alfred (St. Lucia) and Noah Lyles (USA) won the women’s and men’s 100m races. Data from each of their final 100m races is included in the table below:

  1. Note that each one did not immediately start running. The first recorded time is their reaction time \(t_0\). How can this be factored into our model?
  2. The data from the race compares distance vs. time. Using your answer to Problem 3b, find a formula for the distance a sprinter had run, \(x(t)\) (in m), \(t\) seconds into the race for \(t>t_0\).
  3. Alfred’s reaction time was \(0.14\)s and Lyles’ was \(0.18\)s. Their race data has been loaded into Desmos here. Using your general formula for \(x(t)\) and the curve fitting tool in Desmos, determine the values of \(P\) and \(k\) that best fit the data.
TipAnswer:

  1. Our model assumes the sprinter starts running (i.e. \(v=0\) and accelerating according to our equation) at \(t=0\). In reality, each sprinter’s clock starts at the gun, but they don’t begin accelerating until their reaction time \(t_0\) has elapsed. We can account for this by shifting our solution in time: it’s valid starting at \(t=t_0\) rather than \(t=0\), so wherever \(t\) appears in our formula from Discussion Problem 3b, we should use \(t-t_0\) (the time since the sprinter started running) in its place.

  2. Since \(x(t) = \int v(t)\,dt\), and (using our answer to part a) \(v(t) = \dfrac{P}{k}\left(1-e^{-k(t-t_0)}\right)\) for \(t>t_0\), with \(x(t_0)=0\): \[x(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t}\frac{P}{k}\left(1-e^{-k(s-t_0)}\right)\,ds = \frac{P}{k}(t-t_0) - \frac{P}{k}\left[-\frac{1}{k}e^{-k(s-t_0)}\right]_{t_0}^{t}\] \[x(t) = \frac{P}{k}(t-t_0) - \frac{P}{k^2}\left(1-e^{-k(t-t_0)}\right), \quad t>t_0\]

  3. Plugging each sprinter’s reaction time \(t_0\) into the formula from part b and using Desmos’s curve-fitting/regression tool against the \((t,x)\) data provided, you should be able to obtain a best-fit pair of values \((P,k)\) for each sprinter. Since the exact fitted values depend on the details of your regression setup, you may not get precisely the same numbers as your classmates — but you should find that Lyles’ fitted values produce a larger terminal velocity \(P/k\) than Alfred’s, consistent with him running the faster of the two races.
